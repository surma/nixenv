# Language and mechanics

Use this reference for grammar, punctuation, spelling, dates, numbers, units, and inline formatting.

## Voice, person, and tense

Use active voice unless the object matters more than the actor. Name the actor when readers need that information.

Passive voice is acceptable when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately de-emphasized.

Use present tense for behavior and expected results. Use future tense only for an event that occurs later than another stated event.

Do not use future tense for unreleased product behavior.

Address the reader as *you*. Use *user* only for a person who uses software that the reader develops.

Use an imperative for direct instructions. The implied subject is *you*.

Use *we* only for the authoring organization. Establish a clear organizational antecedent before the pronoun.

In reference documentation, describe what an element does. For example, write *Creates a record*, not *Create a record*.

Do not assign human perception or intent to software. Use verbs such as *detects*, *returns*, *specifies*, or *rejects*.

## Sentence order

Put a condition before the result or instruction. This order lets readers skip an instruction that does not apply.

Put a goal before the action when the goal helps the reader understand the action.

Put the action location before the action. For example, name the application before the command within that application.

Keep the sentence subject, verb, and object close together.

Use a second sentence when a parenthetical thought becomes long or important.

Do not put critical information in parentheses.

## Pronouns

Make every pronoun antecedent clear. Replace an ambiguous pronoun with its noun.

Add a noun after *this*, *that*, *these*, or *those* when the noun improves clarity.

Use singular *they* with a plural verb for a person of unspecified gender.

Use *that* for a restrictive clause without a comma. Use *which* for a nonrestrictive clause after a comma.

Use *who* for people when it fits naturally. Use *whose* for people, animals, or things.

Include optional pronouns such as *that* and *which* when omission creates ambiguity.

## Articles and prepositions

Include *a*, *an*, and *the* where standard English requires them.

Choose *a* or *an* by the next word's spoken sound, not its first written letter.

Do not use *the* before a product name. Use *the* before a tool, API, console, or CLI name.

End a sentence with a preposition when that position produces the clearest sentence.

Include a preposition when it clarifies a relationship. Remove a preposition when it adds no meaning.

## Abbreviations

Use an abbreviation only when it saves readers time. Avoid abbreviations that distract from the document's main topic.

For an unfamiliar abbreviation, spell out the term at first mention. Put the abbreviation immediately after it in parentheses.

Italicize both parts when you introduce an abbreviation in ordinary prose.

Capitalize the long form only when it is a proper noun or convention requires capitals.

Use only the abbreviation after its introduction. Do not include a one-use abbreviation unless readers recognize it better than the long form.

Do not expand familiar forms when the expansion adds no value. Common examples include AI, API, HTML, PDF, RAM, REST, URL, and XML.

If the first use occurs in a heading, define the abbreviation in the first paragraph after the heading.

Do not create an abbreviation for local convenience.

Do not use an abbreviation as a verb.

Do not put periods between letters in an acronym or initialism.

Add a period to a shortened word when convention requires it. Do not add a period to an ordinary short form such as *app*.

Form an abbreviation's plural with *s* or *es*. Do not use an apostrophe.

Do not pluralize an abbreviated measurement after a numeral. Write `64 GB`, not `64 GBs`.

Avoid *e.g.* and *i.e.*. Use *for example* and *that is*.

Avoid internet abbreviations and informal shortcuts.

## Contractions

Use familiar two-word contractions to support a natural tone. Negation contractions also make a negative easier to notice.

Do not invent contractions. Do not use three-word contractions.

Use explicit emphasis only when the negative requires unusual attention.

## Singulars, plurals, and possessives

Use ordinary US English plural forms. Do not form a plural with an apostrophe.

Use a plural after *one or more*. Use a singular after *more than one*.

Make the long form and abbreviation agree in number.

Do not put an optional plural in parentheses. Choose one number or write *one or more*.

Do not manually pluralize a class or code name. Add a plural common noun, such as *objects* or *instances*.

For a singular possessive, including a noun ending in *s*, add *'s*.

For a plural ending in *s*, add only an apostrophe. For another plural, add *'s*.

Rewrite an awkward possessive with *of* or another direct construction.

Do not make a product name, feature name, trademark, or code item possessive. Make the following common noun possessive instead.

## Capitalization

Use standard US English capitalization. Do not capitalize a term only to make it seem important.

Use sentence case for titles, headings, navigation, captions, labels, table content, glossary entries, and list items.

In sentence case, capitalize the first word, proper nouns, and officially capitalized terms.

After a colon, use lowercase unless the following text starts a heading, quotation, proper noun, or labeled notice.

Do not use all caps or internal capitals except in official names, abbreviations, placeholders, and exact code.

Do not depend on capitalization alone to distinguish concepts.

Do not name a capitalization pattern with informal labels such as *camel case*. State the pattern and show an example.

## Punctuation principles

Use punctuation to clarify structure. Rewrite a sentence when punctuation must carry too much meaning.

Use straight double quotation marks and straight apostrophes. Use single quotation marks only inside a quotation or in code.

Use one space after a sentence.

### Commas

Use the serial comma before the final *and* or *or* in a series.

Put a comma after an introductory word or phrase.

Put a comma before a coordinating conjunction that joins two independent clauses. Omit it when both clauses are very short.

Do not put a comma between a shared subject and its compound predicate.

Put a comma before a nonrestrictive *which* clause.

Put a semicolon, period, or dash before a conjunctive adverb. Put a comma after that adverb.

Do not normally put a comma before a causal *because* clause.

### Colons

Use a colon to introduce closely related information.

Before a list, the text before the colon must form a complete sentence.

Start ordinary text after a colon with lowercase. Apply the capitalization exceptions listed earlier.

Use a colon or period between a run-in heading and its description. Do not use a dash.

### Dashes and hyphens

Use an em dash for a strong interruption. Do not put spaces around it.

Do not substitute a hyphen, double hyphen, or en dash for an em dash.

Do not use en dashes. Use a hyphen for a numeric range or use the word *to*.

Use a hyphen when it prevents a misreading or joins words that readers must interpret as one modifier.

Do not normally hyphenate a prefix. Hyphenate after *self* or *cross*, before capitals or numbers, and when clarity requires it.

Prefer closed compound nouns when established usage supports them. Check the project style, this skill, and Merriam-Webster in that order.

Hyphenate a compound modifier before a noun when the hyphen improves clarity.

Do not hyphenate an `-ly` adverb with the word it modifies.

Do not normally hyphenate a compound after a verb. Preserve terms that convention always hyphenates.

Hyphenate a number and a spelled-out unit when they modify a noun. Do not hyphenate an abbreviated unit.

Use suspended hyphens for modifiers that share a base, such as *one- or two-hour intervals*.

Do not put spaces around a hyphen.

### Parentheses

Use parentheses sparingly. Keep parenthetical text short and nonessential.

Put a full independent sentence's period inside its parentheses. Put a sentence period outside a closing parenthesis for a partial parenthetical.

Do not use parentheses for optional plurals or optional steps.

### Quotation marks

Use quotation marks for direct quotations and titles of short works. Use italics for titles of full-length works.

Put commas and periods inside quotation marks in ordinary prose.

For a quoted literal string, put punctuation outside unless the punctuation belongs to the literal.

Do not put quotation marks around code-formatted text unless the marks are part of the code.

### Semicolons, slashes, ellipses, and exclamation points

Avoid semicolons. Use one only for closely related clauses or a complex series when simpler punctuation fails.

Avoid slashes in prose. Use words to express alternatives and rates.

Do not use *and/or*. State the available combinations directly.

Do not write dates or fractions with slashes.

Avoid ellipses in prose. Use three periods only for a documented omission or command syntax that requires them.

Do not reproduce a UI label's trailing ellipsis unless omission creates confusion.

Never use exclamation points in concept or reference documentation. Avoid them in procedures.

Use an exclamation point only when syntax, a literal, or a rare learning milestone requires it.

## Examples in prose

Use *such as*, *for example*, or *like* to introduce an example.

Put a comma after *for example* when it starts or interrupts a sentence.

Use commas, parentheses, or an em dash to separate a short example. Use a separate sentence for a long example.

Do not combine a nonexhaustive introduction with *etc.* or *and so on*.

## Text formatting

Use bold for named UI elements and run-in headings. Do not use bold for ordinary emphasis.

Use italics sparingly for a newly defined term, a word discussed as a word, a full-length work, or semantic emphasis.

Use code font for code entities, exact input, exact output, filenames, paths, commands, status codes, and placeholders.

Reserve underlining for links.

Do not override the document's font family, size, or color in source content.

Use semantic markup instead of visual styling when the format supports it.

## Dates and times

Spell out the month and use a four-digit year. Write `January 19, 2017`.

When the weekday matters, place it first. Write `Tuesday, April 27, 2021`.

Do not add a comma between a month and year. Add a comma after a full date within a sentence.

Do not abbreviate months or weekdays unless space is constrained. Use consistent three-letter abbreviations without periods when necessary.

Avoid numeric-only dates. When a system requires one, use ISO order as `YYYY-MM-DD`.

Put the date before the time when both appear.

Use a 12-hour clock unless the product or interface requires 24-hour time.

Write round hours without minutes. Use uppercase `AM` and `PM` after one space.

Use a hyphen without spaces for a time range.

Avoid time zones unless readers require one. Spell out a region-specific zone and include its UTC offset.

Do not use seasons for dates. Use months, quarters, or relevant temperature periods.

## Phone numbers

Use a nonbreaking hyphen to keep each displayed phone number on one line.

For a North American number, separate the area code, exchange code, and four-digit number with nonbreaking hyphens.

For another international number, put `+` directly before the country code. Include the country and area codes.

For an extension, add a comma, the word *extension*, and the extension number.

Use a reserved US example number from `800-555-0100` through `800-555-0199`. Never put a real phone number in an example.

## Numbers

Spell out cardinal numbers from zero through nine. Use numerals for 10 and greater.

Use numerals below 10 for technical quantities, versions, pages, chapters, steps, prices, measurements, percentages, and mathematical values.

Use numerals consistently when one sentence mixes values below and above 10.

Spell out a number at the start of a sentence. Prefer a rewrite that moves a long number away from the start.

Spell out ordinal numbers in prose, such as *first* and *twelfth*.

Use a leading zero for a decimal below one. Treat decimal quantities as plural.

Use a percent sign without a space after a numeral. Spell out a percentage that starts a sentence.

Use a comma every three digits to the left of the decimal point. Use a period as the decimal separator.

Do not group digits to the right of a decimal point.

Use a hyphen without spaces for a simple numeric range. Use *from X to Y*, not *from X-Y*.

Write dimensions with a lowercase `x` and no spaces, such as `192x192`.

Use Arabic numerals instead of Roman numerals unless a constrained nested list requires Roman numerals.

Use decimal fractions when practical. Hyphenate a fraction written as words.

Connect a numerical concept to its practical effect. Link to pricing or sizing information when readers need it.

## Units and currency

Put a nonbreaking space between a numeral and an abbreviated unit, such as `64&nbsp;GB`.

Do not put a space before a percent sign, currency symbol, or angle degree symbol.

For temperature, put a nonbreaking space before the degree symbol. Do not put a space before `C` or `F`.

For kelvin, omit the degree symbol and use a nonbreaking space before `K`.

Repeat a symbol or abbreviated unit for both ends of a range. Use *to* between measured values.

Use *per* for rates when space permits. Reserve a slash for constrained displays or established abbreviations.

Identify ambiguous currency with a country indicator, such as `US$10`.

Use the same decimal or binary byte system as the documented technology. Distinguish `MB` from `MiB` and `GB` from `GiB`.

Use lowercase `k` without a space only for an informal thousands abbreviation. Add a noun that explains the measured item.

## Mathematical notation

Use semantic mathematical symbols or HTML entities when the output format supports them.

Use a true minus sign for subtraction. Do not use a hyphen as a minus sign.

Italicize variables. Do not italicize operators.

Put nonbreaking spaces around operators in an inline expression.

Use `<sup>` for exponents and `<sub>` for subscripts. Do not use a caret for an exponent in prose.

Use notation when it remains grammatical and clear. Use words when notation creates ambiguity.

Use a dedicated renderer or an accessible figure for complex multiline equations. Provide an equivalent explanation.
